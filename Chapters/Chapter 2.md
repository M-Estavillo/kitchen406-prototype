**CHAPTER 2**  
**REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE AND SYSTEMS**

This chapter presents a review of existing literature and systems that are functionally relevant to the proposed system. Each system is examined with respect to its core capabilities, its relevance to the food and small business domain, and the specific functional limitations that justify the distinct features proposed in the Kitchen406 system.

**2.1** **Related Literature**

**2.1.1 E-commerce Adoption for Small Food Businesses**

Digital order intake and inventory tracking have both been shown to directly address the operational strain that manual processes place on small food businesses. In a case study of Nasi Cia, a small culinary startup whose manual order-taking process could not keep pace with rising order volume, researchers documented recurring operational problems: long customer queues, no centralized way to track incoming orders, and a costly dependence on third-party delivery platforms simply to manage order intake (Anugrah & Istiono, 2024). After the business adopted a purpose-built online ordering system, these operational issues were addressed directly, with 91.4% of surveyed users rating the resulting system favorably (Anugrah & Istiono, 2024).

A parallel strain shows up on the inventory side of small food operations. At Kava Coffee Shop, a small culinary business in Kudus, Indonesia, paper-based stock records led to inaccurate counts, reordering that lagged behind actual need, and no way to flag depleting ingredients, leaving the business exposed to both stockouts and overstocking of perishable raw materials (Taufiq et al., 2025). Replacing this manual process with a web-based inventory system built around safety stock and reorder point calculations and minimum-stock alerts tested to a 100% functional success rate, giving the business a way to keep raw material levels accurate and available without relying on staff memory or physical counts (Taufiq et al., 2025).

This reflects a problem structurally identical to Kitchen406's own situation: a home-based operation currently taking orders through informal, message-based channels, with no centralized system for tracking what has been ordered, by whom, or when it is due, and no mechanism linking those orders to the ingredient stock needed to fulfill them. This is a gap that a dedicated ordering and inventory platform is designed to close regardless of its downstream effect on sales.

**2.1.2 Prepaid Subscription Models in Commerce**

In a large-scale field experiment offering roughly two million newspaper readers either an auto-renewing or a non-auto-renewing promotional subscription and tracking them for two years, auto-renewing contract takers were about seven times more likely to continue into a paid subscription after the promotional period than those on non-auto-renewing contracts, reflecting strong consumer inertia around cancellation (Miller et al., 2023). However, the same study found that offering an auto-renewing contract measurably suppressed sign-ups in the first place, where 24% to 36% of potential subscribers avoided subscribing at all when the offer would auto-renew, apparently anticipating their own future. Over the full two-year period, the auto-renewing offer produced roughly 10% fewer total paying subscribers than the non-auto-renewing offer did (Miller et al., 2023). The study further noted that many of the subscribers retained through auto-renewal rarely used the service they were paying for, suggesting that short-term retention gained through a passive default can come at the cost of a smaller and less engaged subscriber base overall (Miller et al., 2023). For a fixed-term, non-auto-renewing model like Kitchen406's, this suggests that requiring customers to actively choose to re-enroll is a structure that plausibly works in the business's favor.

Prepaid subscription models in food commerce have been shown to reshape how consumers commit to and engage with a provider before goods are delivered. In a study of restaurant subscription platforms in Taiwan, prepayment-based subscription access to surplus food was found to depend on consumers' perceived usefulness and ease of use of the platform, with a more favorable attitude toward the subscription translating into a stronger intention to use it and, in turn, more favorable environmental purchasing behavior (Ma & Chang, 2024). This suggests that consumer willingness to commit to a food subscription in advance is closely tied to how clearly the platform communicates value and how little friction it introduces at the point of commitment, a consideration directly relevant to Kitchen406's own prepaid model: fixed-term, non-auto-renewing subscriptions.

This model specific to small food producers remain comparatively understudied, where the closest available reference is Community Supported Agriculture (CSA), where members pay upfront for a defined share of a farm's production over a set season, receive periodic deliveries for the duration of that term, and must actively choose to re-enroll once the term ends rather than being carried over automatically. A survey of those CSA subscription members found that renewal decisions and subscriber retention in a fixed-term model cannot be assumed and must be actively earned each cycle (Chen et al., 2019). While CSA operates on a seasonal, once-a-year renewal cycle rather than the four-week cycle Kitchen406 plans to use, the underlying structure, prepayment for a bounded term followed by an active, unprompted decision to return, is the same mechanism, and the comparative lack of literature on shorter, food-retail-specific cycles is itself a gap this study's documentation may help address.

**2.1.3** **Production Capacity Management Systems**

Production capacity management becomes especially difficult for small and medium-sized bakeries because baking activities are constrained by shared, limited resources such as oven capacity and staff time, and a schedule that minimizes total production time does not automatically minimize idle resource time. In a study modeling three real production lines from small and medium-sized bakeries, a scheduling model built around the constraints most common in bakery settings found that the schedule with the shortest overall completion time was not always the schedule with the least oven idle time, meaning bakeries must actively balance these two goals rather than optimizing for speed alone (Babor et al., 2023). 

This reflects a broader principle established in the production scheduling literature. Because a business' capacity is finite, aligning it with incoming demand may require that some orders be turned away or scheduled deliberately rather than accepted as they arrive, since each order carries both revenue and a processing cost that must be weighed against the business's ability to fulfill it (Slotnick, 2011). Reviewing two decades of research on this order acceptance and scheduling problem, Slotnick (2011) frames the central decision facing capacity-constrained producers as twofold: which orders to accept, and how to schedule the orders that are accepted. Together, these findings support the case for Kitchen406's order calendar. Without a system that enforces defined capacity limits at the point of booking, a bakery managing overlapping subscription cycles and made-to-order requests has no reliable way to know when it has already reached its daily or weekly production ceiling, or which orders should take priority when that ceiling is reached.

**2.1.4 Recipe-Based Inventory Management**

For small, home-based food businesses, recipe and ingredient costing is frequently handled through informal, memory-based methods rather than a structured system, which leaves businesses vulnerable to underpricing as ingredient costs shift. A qualitative case study of eight small-scale kakanin vendors in Pagadian City, Philippines, found that these home-based sellers computed direct ingredient costs primarily through habitual estimation and itemized manual calculation, shifting to more precise, written or app-assisted computation only when bulk orders, new products, or rising prices made errors too costly to risk (Antenero et al., 2026). The same study found that indirect costs such as gas, electricity, and packaging were typically absorbed into pricing through rough percentage-based allocations rather than exact costing, with vendors periodically adjusting their prices upward as overhead costs rose rather than recalculating costs systematically for each batch (Antenero et al., 2026). The authors concluded that these vendors would benefit from simple, standardized costing tools, such as recipe costing sheets or templates, to reduce their reliance on memory and estimation and to protect their margins more reliably. These findings closely mirror the situation described for Kitchen406, where pricing and inventory are currently managed at an aggregate level rather than per product. This supports the case for a recipe-driven inventory system that not only auto-deducts ingredients per confirmed order to maintain accurate stock levels, but also utilizes that real-time consumption data to automatically compute ingredient costs and flag margins, replacing the owners' reliance on mental estimation.

**2.1.5 Inventory Tracking for Bakeries**

Inventory tracking remains a persistent operational challenge for small food manufacturing businesses, including bakeshops, many of which continue to rely on manual, non-automated stock management. A descriptive-comparative study of 200 food manufacturing SMEs in Davao del Sur, Philippines, drawn from five sub-sectors including bakeshops, examined four commonly used inventory strategies: Just-in-Time, ABC Analysis, Safety Stock, and Hybrid Inventory Systems (Adalim et al., 2025). While inventory managers across all four strategies reported generally satisfactory to very satisfactory perceptions of their effect on business performance, statistical analysis found no significant difference in actual business outcomes among the strategies, suggesting that effectiveness depends less on which strategy is adopted and more on how consistently and appropriately it is implemented (Adalim et al., 2025). The study further noted that bakeshops in the region often struggle to move past manual inventory handling, which limits their ability to adopt more advanced, technology-supported tracking methods common among larger firms. These findings suggest that for a small, two-person operation like Kitchen406, the specific inventory strategy chosen matters less than simply having a structured, consistently applied system in place, reinforcing the value of a platform that automatically projects ingredient consumption from confirmed orders rather than relying on visual inspection and ad hoc restocking.

Beyond the choice of inventory strategy, digitizing raw material tracking itself has been shown to directly reduce waste and improve cost control for small food and beverage businesses. In a case study of Cafe Rengganis, a small Indonesian food and beverage business that previously relied on manual recording for its raw materials, transactions, and financial reporting, the adoption of an integrated digital management information system reduced raw material wastage by 29.17 percent and shortened the time needed to prepare financial reports by up to 60 percent (Sari et al., 2024). The study attributed these gains to the system's ability to keep raw material records synchronized with actual usage in real time, which reduced the discrepancies between recorded and physical stock that had previously led to spoilage, overordering, and inaccurate cost figures under the manual process (Sari et al., 2024). This finding directly supports the design of Kitchen406's inventory module, where by automatically projecting and deducting ingredient consumption from confirmed orders rather than relying on manual recording, the system is positioned to produce the same kind of measurable reduction in ingredient waste, tighter control over material costs, and fewer stockouts that digitized tracking delivered for a comparably small food business.

**2.1.6 Social Media Marketing for Small Businesses**

Food and beverage enterprises make up a substantial share of the small businesses in the Philippines that actively rely on social media to reach customers. In a study of 68 small business owners in San Isidro, Nueva Ecija, a sample restricted specifically to businesses already engaged in social media marketing, food and beverage operators accounted for a quarter of respondents, trailing only retail (Santos et al., 2024). Within this group, Facebook was the platform used almost universally, and product photos and promotional posts were the content types owners posted most often reporting stronger customer engagement than those who posted less consistently (Santos et al., 2024).

This gap is consistent with broader findings on Philippine small enterprises, where social media marketing was shown to meaningfully improve brand awareness and customer engagement, but owners' limited financial resources and limited technical skills remained persistent barriers to using it effectively (Santos et al., 2024). Taken together, these findings indicate that the barrier for small food businesses like Kitchen406 is rarely a lack of awareness that marketing matters, but the practical difficulty of sustaining it without dedicated staff or specialized skill, a gap that directly motivates providing the owners with a tool that lowers the effort required to produce marketing content themselves.

**2.1.7** **Cost-Based Pricing with Approval System**

Cost-based pricing, in which a business calculates its production cost and adds a markup, remains a common approach among small and medium-sized enterprises because it is simple, transparent, and requires no specialized market research to apply, unlike value-based pricing, which depends on data the business often does not have the resources to collect (Stromeyer & Kurz, 2025). The same study cautions that more sophisticated, fully automated pricing models can become time and cost prohibitive for small businesses, since they demand data analytics capability and continuous adjustment that resource-constrained owners typically lack, and instead recommends simplified, modular pricing tools that reduce this burden without requiring the business to cede control of the decision (Stromeyer & Kurz, 2025). When participants were given the option to delegate pricing decisions to an algorithm, they were significantly more willing to adopt it when they could override its recommendations than when the algorithm's decisions were final, indicating a general preference for retaining final say even when a computational tool is available to assist the decision (Normann et al., 2025). 

Taken together, these findings support a pricing design in which the system computes a suggested price directly from the ingredient costs the owner has logged during restocking, rather than from broader market or demand data the business does not track, and presents that suggestion for the owner's review rather than applying it automatically. This preserves the transparency and low overhead of cost-based pricing while ensuring the final pricing decision remains with the person who understands the business's full context. 

**2.1.8 Upfront Digital Payments for Small Businesses**

Requiring payment at the point of order, rather than allowing payment on delivery or informal down payment arrangements, has been shown to directly reduce the rate at which customers cancel or abandon orders after placing them. In a study applying machine learning models to real e-commerce transaction data to identify the variables that best predict product returns, cash on delivery was found to be a significant driver of higher return and cancellation rates, since customers who have not yet paid retain little financial commitment to following through once an order has already been placed (Mishra & Dutta, 2024). Based on this finding, the authors recommended that businesses reduce their reliance on cash on delivery and instead incentivize upfront digital payment as a direct way to lower return and cancellation rates. For a small, home-based bakery like Kitchen406, where ingredients are often purchased or prepared specifically for a confirmed order, a cancelled or abandoned order after the fact represents a direct loss of ingredient cost and labor that cannot be recovered. Requiring digital payment upfront, rather than accepting cash on delivery or informal partial down payments as is common in the current message-based ordering process, gives the business the same protection this study identifies: it converts a customer's stated intent to purchase into a confirmed financial commitment before any resources are spent fulfilling the order. 

**2.1.9 Analytics for Small Food Businesses**

Effective analytics for small food and beverage businesses depends less on the sophistication of the underlying model and more on identifying which data actually drives useful decisions. In a case study developing a sales forecasting system for an owner-operated food and beverage outlet, the most influential predictors of sales were found to be time-based patterns, specifically the hour of the day and the day of the week, followed by external factors such as promotional activity, holidays, or local events (Groene & Zakharov, 2024). The study further found that sales data broken down at this level of granularity allowed managers to move from relying on personal experience toward decisions grounded in the business's own historical patterns, directly supporting tasks such as anticipating demand peaks and planning production or staffing around them (Groene & Zakharov, 2024). This suggests that for Kitchen406's analytics dashboard, the most valuable reports are clear views of the business's own order history where sales broken down by day of week and time period, performance by product, and trends over time, can give the owners the same kind of pattern-level visibility that this study identified as the primary driver of better demand-related decisions for a comparably small, owner-operated food business.

While more sophisticated analytics platforms exist, research on how small businesses actually use data suggests that Kitchen406's dashboard should prioritize simplicity and direct usability over technical sophistication. In a qualitative study interviewing 50 small and medium-sized businesses in the United States about their business analytics practices, most reported relying on straightforward, low-cost tools rather than dedicated business intelligence platforms, citing limited budget and a lack of specialized data skills as the primary barriers to adopting more advanced systems (Kasiri et al., 2024). This reinforces that the value of an analytics dashboard for a business like Kitchen406 lies in making the business's own transaction data legible at a glance. This means surfacing figures the owners generate through daily operations, such as total sales volume and order quantity over a given period, sales broken down by individual product to identify which items are performing well, and trends across time periods such as day of week, echoing the granular, time-based sales patterns identified as most operationally useful in Groene and Zakharov's (2024) study of small food and beverage forecasting. A dashboard built around this kind of direct, order-derived reporting gives the owners visibility into their own sales history without requiring the technical investment that smaller businesses in the Kasiri et al. study consistently struggled to justify.

**2.1.10 AI-Generated Marketing Posts**

Generating marketing content with AI does not remove the owner from the process. It changes what the owner has to do to direct it. A formative study of six small business owners in the United Kingdom found that unstructured, free-text prompting was a significant source of friction for non-technical users, who struggled to articulate their brand intuition and creative goals in a way generative AI tools could act on, often resulting in generic content that did not reflect what they actually wanted (Karnatak et al., 2025). In response, the researchers developed a structured input system that let business owners convey branding, audience, and goal information through defined fields rather than open-ended text alone, and found that this structure improved user agency and control, produced outputs more consistent with the owner's intended brand vision, and made the tool more approachable for owners without a design or marketing background (Karnatak et al., 2025). This finding directly informs the design of Kitchen406's post generation feature. The Kitchen406 marketing assistant does not rely on open-ended prompting from the owner. Instead, draft generation is triggered automatically by predefined events, while relevant operational data from the system is used as context for the generated content. The owner does not initiate individual drafts; instead, the owner's role begins after generation, when the draft is reviewed and either approved or rejected. The AI's role, and its access to the system's live data, is to execute that direction accurately rather than to generate content independently.

However, generated content carries a risk if it is not connected to the business's actual data. Posts can drift out of sync with what the business can currently offer, describing products, prices, or availability that no longer reflect reality. A systematic literature review of generative AI in digital marketing found that GAI-driven tools consistently outperformed conventional content creation when their output was grounded in the business's own operational data, producing more personalized and contextually relevant content than generic, manually written alternatives (Salih et al., 2026). The review attributed this advantage to data-driven generation staying aligned with real conditions, unlike static, pre-written templates that require manual updating whenever underlying details change (Salih et al., 2026). This supports pulling Kitchen406's post generation directly from the system's live data, such as current product availability, pricing, and order status, rather than requiring the owner to manually re-enter this information each time, reducing the risk of advertising a product that is out of stock or a price that has since changed.

Consumer skepticism toward AI-generated content is well documented, but this negative reaction is not fixed. Two controlled experiments testing how audiences respond to different forms of human-AI collaboration in content creation found that content labeled as purely AI-generated, or as AI-supported human writing, was rated significantly less credible than human-authored content (Haupt et al., 2025). However, content explicitly framed as AI-generated but human-controlled, meaning a person reviewed the output and held final responsibility for it before it was published, was not rated differently from fully human-authored content on either credibility or company attitude (Haupt et al., 2025). The researchers attributed this to a restored sense of control. Audiences reacted negatively to AI authorship mainly when no human oversight was indicated, since the presence of a human decision-maker reassured them that someone remained accountable for what was published. This finding is directly relevant to Kitchen406's post generation feature.  Because each draft is generated in response to a predefined business event but remains subject to owner review and approval before publication, the feature still follows a human-controlled AI authorship model.

**2.2 Related Systems**

**2.2.1 E-commerce Platforms with Subscription Models**

Subbly

Several commercial platforms already address recurring billing at scale, such as Subbly is a subscription-first e-commerce system that validates the core mechanics of online storefronts, automated payments, and customer self-service portals. Subbly successfully process prepaid commitments and provide a mechanism for customers to defer or skip upcoming deliveries. However, because Subbly is a general-purpose Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) product built to accommodate a wide public market, it inherently relies on standard inventory models rather than the ingredient-level tracking required by a micro-scale food producer. Rather than competing with Subbly's broad enterprise feature set, Kitchen406 addresses these specific operational gaps by acting as a bespoke, single-business solution, tailored exclusively to the unique workflows and strict capacity constraints of its owners.

BreadHive

While software platforms demonstrate the technical mechanics of recurring billing, existing small-scale bakeries provide the operational blueprint for prepaid food subscriptions. BreadHive, an independent bakery, successfully implements a “Bread Club” model where customers prepay for a weekly share of baked goods collected during a strict, fixed fulfillment window. Like the proposed Kitchen406 system, BreadHive secures commitments in advance and accommodates skipped weeks when scheduled beforehand, ensuring a predictable production schedule. However, because BreadHive’s online storefront treats subscriptions as standard product sales, it relies on manual operational calculation for inventory, whereas Kitchen406 structurally addresses this gap by integrating a dynamic Bill of Materials (BoM) to automatically project and deduct raw ingredient consumption across all orders.

**2.2.3 Bakery-Specific Inventory Platforms**

Cybake

	Cybake is a UK-based bakery management software offering production planning, recipe management, and order processing. Supporting bakery operations from production planning through fulfillment is the primary focus of Cybake, a platform designed for retail and wholesale bakeries. The system links recipe information directly to production requirements, allowing ingredient consumption and procurement needs to be calculated from planned production quantities (Cybake Ltd., 2025). It also combines order processing, production management, inventory control, and delivery coordination within a single environment. A notable feature of the platform is its demand-driven planning approach, which can use sales data to forecast production requirements and adjust output accordingly (Cybake Ltd., 2025). This demonstrates how recipe, inventory, and production information can be integrated to support operational decision-making. However, Kitchen406 operates on a made-to-order model where production is driven by confirmed customer orders and subscription commitments rather than retail sales forecasts. Consequently, the proposed system emphasizes order-based production planning while applying similar bill-of-materials principles to project ingredient requirements and monitor stock levels.

Yokitup

Reducing the gap between incoming orders and production activities is a central objective of Yokitup. The platform presents confirmed orders in a production-oriented view, helping bakery owners determine what needs to be prepared without manually translating order records into baking schedules (Yokitup, 2026). It also supports recipe-based inventory management, stock monitoring, and cost analysis, allowing businesses to estimate ingredient requirements and track production costs (Yokitup, 2026). These capabilities illustrate how operational data can be connected to day-to-day bakery production. Unlike Yokitup, however, Kitchen406 is designed as a broader operational platform that combines online ordering, subscription management, capacity control, inventory monitoring, and business analytics within a single system. Rather than treating inventory and production as separate operational functions, the proposed platform links them directly to customer orders and recurring subscription commitments.

**2.2.4 AI Assistants for Marketing**

Marky AI

	Unlike enterprise-oriented marketing platforms, Marky AI focuses on helping small businesses maintain an active social media presence with minimal effort. The platform analyzes information from a business's website and online profile to generate branded posts that can be prepared in advance and scheduled for publication across multiple social media platforms. By automating much of the content creation, Marky AI reduces the time required for businesses to create marketing content consistently. Although its target users are comparable to Kitchen406, its approach differs from that of the proposed system. Kitchen406's AI marketing assistant automatically generates draft promotional content when predefined events occur, using operational data already stored within the bakery's platform as context. This allows posts to reflect current products, promotions, and business information without requiring the owners to manually initiate or prompt each draft. Unlike Marky AI, which emphasizes automated publishing workflows, the AI marketing assistant functions solely as a drafting assistant, with all generated content remaining subject to owner review, approval, and publication.

Template AI

	Template AI is a general-purpose AI template generator that offers a wide range of content creation tools, including document, design, presentation, and image generators. Among these, its AI-powered image and photo generation tools allow users to produce marketing visuals, product imagery, and branded graphics directly from a text or voice prompt, without requiring prior design experience or existing photo assets. Users can specify the desired style, lighting, and composition, and the platform generates ready-to-use images that can be customized further with brand colors, fonts, and logos. However, since Template AI generates images purely based on user-provided prompts, its output is not connected to a business's actual products, inventory, or operational data. In contrast, Kitchen406's AI marketing assistant is intended to generate promotional content in connection with information available within the bakery's platform, allowing posts to reflect current products and offerings without the owners needing to manually describe them each time.
